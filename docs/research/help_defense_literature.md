### A.2 FIBA material (relayed, not opened directly)

An aggregator page (hoopsplaybook.ca, "Defence - Help rotations", opened) compiles coach quotes. From it: "help must come from below the ball" (attributed to WABC, i.e. FIBA's coaching manual); Ernie Woods warns that when a high weak-side defender stops middle penetration, a low defender who steps up "creates an easy passing lane to his man"; Allison McNeill says two defenders form a "weakside i" and rotate "using the closest-player rules". A search excerpt of FIBA's WABC Level 1 page 1.1.9 ("Help defence, helping the helper, defensive rotation"; UNVERIFIED, page not opened) said: players must be alert "to help a teammate that is helping to stop a dribbling penetration"; "this is not a 'switching' defence. Each defender ultimately will continue to guard their own player"; for seam penetration, rotating to help the helper "is difficult and this may leave a pass to 5 open"; FIBA recommends 4x4 to teach rotation. Treat these as leads to confirm on the FIBA site, not as citations.

### A.3 Answer to the key question: is a rotation expected after NEAR help, or only after LONG help?

What the opened sources support:

1. **Doctrine expects the second rotation after the first help in general.** None of the opened sources says "no rotation after near/gap help". HoopTactics' own examples of help the helper start from a near, ball-side helper (X3/X4 stepping out, taking a charge or trapping) and then have a weak-side defender drop. Cleaning the Glass says the rotation matters most when the helper comes from near the rim (that is, a low helper), because the helper's man is then a layup, dunk or rebound threat.
2. **The doctrine is about who is open and how dangerous it is, not about the helper's distance as such.** The rotation is demanded when the helper leaves a man who can score quickly. Positional systems (pack line, gap help, nail help at two passes away) are designed so that defenders already stand in help position and may only need to recover, which means less rotation (Basketball For Coaches; HoopStudent). That supports a softer version of the owner's reading: near/gap help in a pack-line style does not necessarily require a rotation, whereas help that pulls a defender off a perimeter shooter does.
3. **The owner's exact claim ("long help implies rotation, near help does not necessarily") is not stated in any opened source.** It is a reasonable inference from (2) and from the X-out article (a long weak-side recovery path vs a short strong-side one), and from Cleaning the Glass ("Teams instead will either rotate more aggressively, or not rotate at all, based on the matchup"). Do not present it as a quotation from doctrine. Present it as the coach's reading, supported by the fact that doctrine ties rotation to the open man's danger and the scheme.
4. **Who rotates is specified as the nearest or the low weak-side defender, not "all three".** In HoopTactics and the closest-player rule, one specific defender drops or covers the helper's man. That has a direct consequence for the entry's measure: averaging the three defenders' projection toward the man left dilutes a one-defender signal (see E.2).
5. **Timing is anchored on the pass.** "All rotations take place as ball leaves passer's hands." The entry's 1.5 s window starts at the helper's step. If the pass has not happened, doctrine says the rotation is anticipatory or absent.

## (C) Methods notes

1. **Placebo / null comparison in tracking.** The entry's contrast (projection toward the man left minus projection toward the other attackers) is a within-event placebo. I found no sports-tracking paper that names this design (the search returned only generic event-study placebo logic, such as "with N placebos, the minimum achievable p-value is 1/(N+1)", from an economics paper, excerpt only, UNVERIFIED). Borrowable ideas: (a) placebo events (same drive geometry, no helper step; or random frames from the same possession) to calibrate the null displacement; (b) a ghosting baseline (Le et al. 2017; Seidl et al.) for the expected defender displacement in that geometry; (c) a permutation test that shuffles which attacker is called "the man left" within each event (this is already close to what the placebo does, and gives an exact p-value).
2. **Pitfall: classifying events by an outcome-related quantity.** The helper is "the nearest off-ball defender" and "does not get back to his own man". If "does not get back" is defined from distances that the other defenders' movement also affects, the classification is conditioned on a post-event variable. General reference: Montgomery, J. M., Nyhan, B., Torres, M. (2018), How Conditioning on Posttreatment Variables Can Ruin Your Experiment and What to Do about It, *American Journal of Political Science* 62(3), 760-775, doi:10.1111/ajps.12357 (opened as a search excerpt and the RePEc record; full text not read). Their point: dropping or subsetting observations on a variable the treatment can affect biases the estimate. Applied here: define the event from information available at the helper's step (his position and velocity relative to the driver), and not from where he ends up after 1.5 s.
3. **Geometry confound.** Everyone moves toward the ball in a drive (Bourbousson et al. found strong in-phase longitudinal coupling). The placebo handles this only if the man left and the other attackers are comparable in geometry (distance to the ball and to the basket). Matching or regression adjustment on those two covariates is the standard remedy.
4. **How others threshold events.** Supola et al.: drive = speed above 1.59 m/s and distance proportion above 0.50, cut = at least 1.81 m/s, thresholds set from about 15-30 annotated actions. Li 2025: 45 deg within 0.4 s, 1.5 m for 3 frames. Lucey et al.: "open" = nearest defender at least 6 ft. Lesson: every published threshold is a hand-set value validated on very few labelled cases, so a sensitivity sweep (several thresholds, report all) is the accepted honest practice and costs little.
5. **Matchups from the provider.** Franks et al. and Keshri et al. show matchups can be inferred from positions alone; cross-checking the provider's assignment against an HMM or a closest-defender baseline would quantify how often "his man" is ambiguous (Franks: "there is often some uncertainty about who's guarding whom near the basket").

## (E) What this changes for the entry

### E.1 Related-work sentence for the README (49 words)

> Help-the-helper rotations are standard coaching doctrine (HoopTactics; Cleaning the Glass) but, to our knowledge, unmeasured from tracking; related work infers matchups (Franks et al. 2015; Keshri et al. 2019), detects drive-time defensive gaps (Supola et al. 2023) and links defensive role swaps to open shots (Lucey et al. 2014).

### E.2 Method improvements, ranked by value over effort

| # | Change | Why (source) | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Test the nearest-to-the-helper's-man defender (and the weak-side low man) separately instead of averaging all three | Doctrine assigns the rotation to one defender (closest-player rule; low man; "the third defender covers the helper's original assignment"). Averaging three dilutes a one-defender effect, which could by itself produce the null | Low |
| 2 | Split the sample near vs long (by the helper's distance from the ball at the step, or by pass distance: one pass away vs two or more) and report both with CIs, even though long has 7 cases | Directly tests the coach's reading; 7 cases can be shown as a descriptive list, not an inference | Low |
| 3 | Re-anchor the window on the pass out of the drive (or give a second window ending at the first pass) | "All rotations take place as ball leaves passer's hands" (HoopTactics): no pass, no expected rotation | Medium |
| 4 | Define the event only from information at the helper's step and run a threshold sweep | Posttreatment-conditioning risk (Montgomery et al. 2018); published thresholds are hand-set on few cases | Low |
| 5 | Adjust or match the placebo on distance to ball and to basket | Avoids a geometry confound (everyone drifts toward the ball) | Medium |
| 6 | Cross-check the provider's matchups with a closest-defender baseline or the Franks HMM; report the share of ambiguous "man left" cases | Franks: uncertainty near the basket; Keshri: baselines differ a lot | Medium |
| 7 | Add a ghost or placebo-event baseline (frames without a helper step) for the displacement null | Le et al. 2017; gives a calibrated "expected" displacement | High (skip unless time) |

### E.3 Framing risks

1. **Doctrine does not say near help requires rotation.** Cleaning the Glass says the rotation matters most when help comes from near the rim (layup threat), HoopTactics starts its help-the-helper drills from a near helper, but positional systems (pack line, gap help) are built so that no rotation is needed beyond recovery, and pro rotation is "based on the matchup". So the absence is compatible with at least three readings: the defenders recover instead of rotating, the rotation is by one specific defender and is diluted by the three-defender average, or the rotation happens after the pass and outside the 1.5 s window. State the absence as "no average pull toward the man left in the 1.5 s after the step", and not as "teams fail to rotate".
2. **The owner's rule ("long help implies rotation") is the coach's reading, not a quoted doctrine** (A.3 point 3). Write it as the hypothesis the long-help subset (7 cases) cannot confirm or reject.
3. **Someone may have measured it.** My searches found no such paper, but the Sloan archive is not full-text searchable here and several Sloan papers were not opened. Use "to our knowledge" and cite Supola et al. 2023 as the nearest work, with the difference stated (assignment-aware, placebo-controlled, no cutting).
4. **Intent language.** Keep "did not move toward", never "chose" or "decided"; Franks et al. also warn that matchups near the basket are uncertain.
5. **Inconsistent secondary sources.** Do not cite Li (2025) as support for any threshold; the paper contradicts itself in several places. Do not cite FIBA directly until the manual page is opened from a machine that resolves wabc.fiba.com.

### E.4 Resumen para el propietario (español)

- **Doctrina.** Todas las fuentes que pude abrir (HoopTactics, Cleaning the Glass, HoopStudent, NBAPlaybook, Ángel Jareño) enseñan "ayudar al que ayuda" como una segunda rotación tras la primera ayuda. Ninguna dice textualmente que "tras ayuda cercana no hace falta rotar y tras ayuda larga sí": esa es una lectura razonable tuya, apoyada en que la rotación depende de lo peligroso que sea el hombre abandonado (Cleaning the Glass: importa más cuando la ayuda viene de cerca del aro) y en que los sistemas posicionales (pack line, ayuda de hueco) están hechos para que baste con recuperar.
- **Quién rota.** La doctrina asigna la rotación a un defensor concreto (el más cercano al hombre libre, o el "low man" del lado débil), no a los tres. Promediar los tres puede diluir el efecto y fabricar la ausencia. Propuesta de mayor valor y menor coste: medir por separado a ese defensor.
- **Cuándo.** El libro de HoopTactics dice que las rotaciones ocurren "cuando el balón sale de las manos del pasador": con ventana de 1,5 s desde el paso del ayudante puede que aún no haya pase.
- **Literatura.** No encontré ningún trabajo que mida la rotación al hombre dejado por el ayudante desde tracking.
